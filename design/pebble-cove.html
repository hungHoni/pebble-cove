Game design proposal · draft 10 · 4 Nov 2026

Pebble Cove

A calm place to take a break, or to keep you company while you work, shared with real people.

A quiet beach in your browser, shared with up to 49 other people. Walk on the sand, wave, sit and watch the water, or add a stone to a cairn. Every 15 minutes the tide comes in and washes the cairns away, and everyone starts again on fresh sand.

Step 1 is built. Next: test it on your iPhone and with five new players.

How a visit goes

  1. ArriveNo account and no install. Pick a color and a hat. On a first visit, a short hint shows the three buttons.
  2. Choose a coveEach cove shows its head count and its next tide. Or open a friend's cove link to join them.
  3. Spend timeWalk, wave, sit and watch the water, or add a stone to a cairn. The beach is four screens wide and the camera follows you.
  4. The tide comes inEvery 15 minutes, at a different time in each cove. You get a postcard of the beach as it was, then the water washes every cairn away.
  5. Fresh sandYour stone total and postcard album stay on this device. Stay for another tide or leave whenever you like.

↻ Steps 3 to 5 repeat for as long as you stay.

Each cove runs one timetable, so events never overlap: the tide every 15 minutes, the great low tide once an hour between two tides, the whale once a day, and gusts at quiet moments. Cairns on the sandbar wash away with the next tide.

Ideas to wow players

Seven moments that make a quiet beach memorable. Each one works without chat, and none needs fast servers. The first is sketched below in the paper cut look.

The whale passes

Step 2

Once a day, at the same second, a whale swims past every cove. Everyone playing sees it together, all 100,000 if that's how many are on. People wave at it without being told to.

Effort
Low: one shared clock and one animation
Why it works
It's the one moment the whole game shares
  • The crowd waveWhen five or more people on the same screen press Wave one after another, a stadium-style wave ripples across them, and the sea answers with a big breaker and a burst of spray.Effort: low to medium
  • Stones that travelWhen the tide takes a cairn, its stones wash up days later in another cove, still in the builder's color. Pick one up and it says where it came from: "Washed in from Cove 47."Effort: medium
  • Cairns that singEvery stone plays a soft note as it lands, tuned so any mix sounds good. Each cove slowly builds its own tune, and when the tide comes in it plays every cairn one last time, low to high.Effort: low, sound made in the browser
  • The great low tideOnce an hour the sea pulls far back for two minutes, uncovering tidepools and a sandbar out to a small island. Everyone walks out together and hurries back before the water returns. Anyone the water catches floats gently back to shore.Effort: low to medium, the same map with a lower water line
  • Living postcardsThe tide postcard becomes a 10-second replay of the beach, stored as a few kilobytes of positions. A replay is saved only when you share it, kept for 90 days, and can be reported. Opening the link takes a friend straight to your cove.Effort: medium
  • Hats in the windNow and then a gust blows across the cove and every hat on the beach lifts and tumbles along the sand. Players chase their own, and hats that land in the water float back on the next wave. Every screen works out the same hat paths, so gusts add no traffic, and only the owner can pick up their hat.Effort: low

On 4 Nov you chose all seven for the first release. The build plan shows which step each one arrives in.

Why people stay and come back

Everything here supports the purpose at the top of this page, at a different timescale, from the next few minutes to the next season.

WhenWhy stay or returnWhat the game does
Right nowIt's calm, and you're not aloneWaves, gulls and people nearby. One tide makes a natural 15-minute break: stay for one tide.
While you workQuiet company in another tabSit, and your character watches the water while you work. A soft chime marks each tide, like a gentle focus timer.
TomorrowSomething to see, and someone to seeThe daily whale at the same time. The matchmaker puts you back in your usual cove, so the same regulars are there.
This monthSomething to collectEach tide washes up a few shells to find, and postcards fill an album. Every ten tides on the beach earns a new hat.
This seasonSomething to build togetherEvery stone anyone stacks adds to a lighthouse on the headland, rising in every cove at once. When it's finished, the season changes the beach.
  • Familiar facesAfter you've waved with someone a few times, a small shell appears above them on your screen to mark a friendly face. There are no names and no chat, only the feeling of seeing a regular.
  • Nothing to loseNo streaks to break and no timers that punish you. Skip a week and the beach is still there, with your shells and postcards waiting.

There is no chat. People talk with waves and stones only, which keeps the cove calm and removes most of the work of keeping it friendly. You can still hide anyone you'd rather not see. Cairns go only on spaced-out spots, so stones can't spell words, and the matchmaker limits how many players can join from one address. Shared postcards can be reported, the cove cam runs a few minutes behind, and each player can only wave and stack so often.

Built for thousands at once

Each cove runs on its own, on one server process. Only a few small, slow things are shared between coves: the whale's clock, travelling stones and the lighthouse total, all through Redis. So twice the players means twice the coves and twice the processes.

Traffic costAt that peak all month, about 40–80 TB. On the big clouds that costs thousands of dollars a month, so use a host that includes traffic in the server price.

Sending each player only the people near them on the beach, not the whole cove, should cut traffic by half or more, which matters most on a big cloud, where traffic is about 85% of the bill. If 100,000 is your total number of players rather than how many play at once, expect a few thousand online at peak: one or two machines, roughly €150–300 a month on a budget host.

Paying for it

Pebble Cove stays free to play. Most visits don't overlap, so even a million visits a month is a small server bill, and a budget cap stops the bill from running away.

A player in a hidden tab gets only tide and whale updates. At all three sizes, traffic stays inside the allowance included with each machine.

  • Support the coveA small button opens a page with a donation link. Supporters get a shell on their hat for a month, and nothing that changes play.
  • Hats and colorsPaid looks for $1–3 each, never anything that changes play. Buying needs a simple account or a restore code, so this comes after launch.
  • A budget capThe matchmaker runs only as many coves as the month's budget pays for. When every cove is full, new visitors wait on a quiet shore until a spot opens. The whale runs on the clock, so it passes the waiting shore too.
  • Outside supportArts grants, free cloud credits for new companies, or a sponsor who keeps one cove open with their name on the sign.

A rough check: 1 million visits might come from about 300,000 people. If 1 in 1,000 of them gives €3, that's about €900 a month, around seven times the server bill at that size. The bigger cost is your own time for updates, support and keeping coves friendly, which no server bill shows.

Getting the word out

The game markets itself in two ways: its moments make short, calm videos, and every postcard link drops a new person onto the beach. The plan spends time, not money.

Before launch

  • A one-page site with a countdown to the first whale and an email sign-up.
  • Short clips while building, like the paper tide sliding in or a cairn singing as the water takes it. Posted to TikTok, Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts.
  • A small Discord for testers, who become the first regulars.

Launch week

  • The first whale: one set time when everyone is invited to watch it together.
  • Posts on itch.io, Reddit (r/WebGames, r/CozyGamers), Hacker News and Product Hunt.
  • Notes to cozy-game newsletters and to streamers who run study streams. A stream mode hides the buttons for a clean view.

After launch

  • A 24/7 cove cam on YouTube: the real beach with real players, as a background for studying. Viewers click through to join.
  • Each season and each finished lighthouse gives something new to post.
  • Early translations. The game has very little text, so Korean, Vietnamese, Japanese and Spanish versions are cheap.

Build plan, once you approve

  1. One beach, one player · built 4 NovWalk, wave, sit and stack in paper cut, with the camera following you along the beach. Cairns that sing, hats in the wind and the sandbar for the great low tide arrive here, because they work without other people. Done when it feels calm and works in Safari on an iPhone.
  2. One live coveThe server runs a cove for 50 players. Other people move smoothly, and the tide and postcard arrive for everyone at the same moment. The whale, the crowd wave and living postcards arrive here, because they need other people.
  3. Many covesMatchmaker, head counts, friend links, a clear message when a cove is full, and automatic reconnect when a phone drops its connection. Stones that travel arrive here, because they move between coves.
  4. Bot test5,000 bot players across 100 coves. Measure update time, CPU and data per player, then fix whatever is slow.
  5. Soft launchDeploy to one region with monitoring. Friends first, then a closed beta, then the public.
  6. After launchShells and the album, hats earned by time on the beach, the lighthouse and seasons, familiar faces, the supporter shell, stream mode and the cove cam. Familiar faces and the supporter shell need a lasting player ID, the first step toward optional accounts.

Step 1 in detail

What step 1 includes

  • One beach, four screens wide, in paper cut, with the camera following you.
  • Pick a color and a hat, then walk, wave, sit and stack.
  • The 15-minute tide: the timer, the water rising and the cairns washing away.
  • A still postcard at each tide, saved to your album on this device.
  • Cairns that sing, hats in the wind, and the great low tide on the same map.
  • Game rules written apart from the drawing code, so the step 2 server can run the same rules.
  • A test hook for your iPhone play-test robot, so it can read the game and press its buttons.

Done when

  • It runs at 60 frames a second on your iPhone in Safari, and never below 30 on an older iPhone.
  • The first screen shows in under 3 seconds on 4G, and the whole game is under 2 MB.
  • It works upright and sideways, with more beach showing sideways.
  • Sound starts on the first tap, as iOS requires, and the mute setting is remembered.
  • Five people who haven't seen it each play one full tide, and at least four say it felt calm.
  • The play-test robot gets through a full tide on the iPhone without errors.

What the review changed

Two independent reviews have read the design. The first read draft 1 and rated paper cut the strongest look. The second read draft 8, after the features were chosen. Everything both reviews raised is fixed in this draft.

First review, draft 1

  • Routing. Draft 1 kept each player on one server with the load balancer, and that server might not be running their cove. Now the matchmaker names the server, and the load balancer routes by its ID.
  • Data and cost. The traffic estimate left out connection overhead and assumed only 20 people moving. It now gives a range and a monthly traffic figure.
  • Crowding. 50 people on one screen isn't calm. Each cove is now a long beach, four screens wide, with a camera that follows you.
  • Reasons to come back. A stone total kept on your device, a postcard at every tide, links to bring a friend into your cove, and a different tide time for each cove.
  • Safety. No chat doesn't mean nothing to moderate. Added hiding a player, spaced-out cairn spots and a limit on joins from one address.
  • Drawing. Your character is no longer behind the buttons, characters are bigger, seated people have faces, and the soft paint look is less see-through.

Second review, draft 8

  • Long visits. Costs are redone for 30-minute visits, a hidden tab gets only tide and whale updates, and three wrong figures are corrected.
  • Budget and the whale. The budget cap now follows the launch size, and the whale passes the waiting shore too.
  • Scope. Extras the page promised but the plan left out are now listed under After launch.
  • One timetable. Each cove runs one timetable, so tides, the low tide, the whale and gusts never overlap.
  • Postcards and abuse. Replays are saved only when shared, kept 90 days and can be reported. The cove cam runs a few minutes behind.
  • Step 1. Now written out in detail, with tests you can check.
